# Comprehensive Quality Assessment of C-TVAE Generated Synthetic Data

## Executive Summary

This document presents a rigorous statistical and visual analysis of synthetic click data generated by our Conditional Tabular Variational Autoencoder (C-TVAE). Through multiple complementary evaluation methodologies including univariate distribution comparisons, correlation structure analysis, multivariate projection techniques, and privacy assessment, we demonstrate that the C-TVAE produces high-quality synthetic data that closely approximates real click data while maintaining novelty and avoiding simple copying. Our analysis reveals a mean Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of 0.1359 across 46 features, indicating good distributional similarity, and an exact match rate of only 0.10%, confirming that synthetic samples represent novel instances rather than duplicates of training data.

---

## 1. Motivation and Methodological Framework

### 1.1 The Critical Importance of Synthetic Data Quality

The effectiveness of synthetic data augmentation for addressing class imbalance fundamentally depends on the quality of the generated samples. While our previous analysis demonstrated that C-TVAE augmented models achieve dramatic performance improvements (Logistic Regression AUC: 0.791 → 0.958; LightGBM AUC: 0.815 → 0.974), these gains could potentially arise from multiple mechanisms, not all of which are equally desirable. In the best case, synthetic data provides genuinely informative examples that help models learn robust decision boundaries by filling gaps in the feature space coverage. In problematic scenarios, synthetic data might introduce artifacts, unrealistic feature combinations, or overly simplistic patterns that lead to overfitting or poor generalization to real-world data.

Distinguishing between these scenarios requires careful quality assessment that goes beyond measuring downstream task performance. A model might perform well on validation data that includes synthetic samples while failing catastrophically on purely real-world data if the synthetic samples contain systematic biases or unrealistic patterns. Conversely, high-quality synthetic data should exhibit statistical properties that closely match real data across multiple dimensions: marginal feature distributions, correlation structures, multivariate relationships, and adherence to domain constraints. Our quality assessment framework employs complementary statistical tests and visualizations to evaluate synthetic data quality across these dimensions, providing confidence that the performance improvements we observe reflect genuine learning rather than artifacts of synthetic data generation.

### 1.2 Evaluation Dimensions and Metrics

Our quality assessment framework evaluates synthetic data across six complementary dimensions, each addressing different aspects of data realism and utility. First, univariate distribution similarity assesses whether individual features in synthetic data follow the same statistical distributions as real data, measured through visual comparison of histograms and formal statistical tests like the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Second, correlation structure preservation evaluates whether the C-TVAE maintains the relationships between features, which is critical because models learn not just from individual features but from their interactions and correlations. Third, multivariate distribution analysis uses dimensionality reduction techniques like Principal Component Analysis to assess whether synthetic data occupies the same regions of high-dimensional feature space as real data.

Fourth, range validation ensures that synthetic features respect the natural bounds and constraints of real data, avoiding impossible or implausible values that would violate domain knowledge. Fifth, privacy and novelty analysis quantifies the degree to which synthetic samples represent genuinely new instances rather than memorized copies of training data, addressing both practical privacy concerns and theoretical questions about whether the generative model has learned to interpolate versus merely memorize. Sixth, statistical hypothesis testing provides formal quantification of distributional similarity through metrics like the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic and Jensen-Shannon divergence, allowing us to make principled statements about whether observed differences between real and synthetic data are statistically significant or within the range of natural sampling variation.

### 1.3 Dataset Composition and Analytical Scope

Our quality analysis compares 15,701 real click instances (extracted from our 1-million-sample training set subsample) against 1,311 synthetic click instances generated by the C-TVAE model trained on targeted segment augmentation (specifically, clicks from city_rank=3). This synthetic dataset represents the targeted augmentation component of our overall C-TVAE strategy, where we identified underrepresented segments and generated synthetic samples to balance their representation. While we also generated a larger set of 93,665 synthetic samples for general augmentation to achieve 10% overall click rate, this analysis focuses on the smaller targeted dataset to enable more detailed statistical comparison and visualization without computational constraints.

The analysis encompasses 46 numeric features derived from the original high-dimensional dataset through preprocessing steps including target encoding of high-cardinality categorical variables and one-hot encoding of low-cardinality categorical features. These features represent a diverse array of information types: user demographics (age, gender, city_rank), behavioral patterns (refresh times, feed lifecycle), historical engagement metrics (feeds impressions, clicks, CTR), network characteristics (net_type), creative attributes (slot_id, device_name), and engineered interaction features (various encoded variables). This diversity is important for our quality assessment because it allows us to evaluate whether the C-TVAE successfully learns to generate realistic patterns across fundamentally different types of features, from simple categorical indicators to complex continuous distributions to engineered features with intricate correlation structures.

## 2. Univariate Distribution Analysis

### 2.1 Visual Comparison of Feature Distributions

Our univariate distribution analysis reveals that the C-TVAE successfully captures the marginal distributions of most features, though with varying degrees of fidelity across different feature types. Visual inspection of histogram overlays for the first 12 features shows strong alignment between real and synthetic distributions for several key variables. For example, the `app_score` feature exhibits nearly identical distributions between real and synthetic data, with both showing a characteristic right-skewed pattern concentrated around values of 8-10. Similarly, features like `pt_d` (a timestamp-derived feature) show distributions that closely track each other, maintaining the same general shape and central tendency despite minor differences in tail behavior.

However, certain features exhibit more substantial distributional differences that merit careful interpretation. The `age` feature, representing user age categories encoded as integers, shows a notable shift in the synthetic distribution toward younger age groups. Specifically, the real data shows a relatively uniform distribution across age categories 3-7, while the synthetic data concentrates more heavily around categories 4-5. This pattern suggests that the C-TVAE has learned that clicks are somewhat more common among middle-age users in the city_rank=3 segment and consequently generates synthetic samples that reflect this learned association. Similarly, `gender_4` (one of the one-hot encoded gender categories) shows a dramatic difference, with synthetic data heavily favoring this category while real data shows much more balance across gender categories.

The interpretation of these distributional differences requires consideration of what the C-TVAE was conditioned on during generation. Because we generated synthetic samples specifically for city_rank=3 clicks, the model draws from the learned distribution of feature values conditional on both label=1 (click) and city_rank=3. If certain age groups or genders are more prevalent among city_rank=3 clickers in the training data, the synthetic distribution will reflect these conditional patterns. This is actually a feature, not a bug, of conditional generation: the synthetic data should capture the specific characteristics of the target segment, not just generic click patterns. However, it does mean that synthetic data may exhibit different marginal distributions than the overall real click population, which is appropriate given the targeted nature of the augmentation.

### 2.2 Statistical Similarity Tests: Kolmogorov-Smirnov Results

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) two-sample test provides a formal statistical framework for assessing whether the distribution of each feature differs significantly between real and synthetic data. The KS test measures the maximum absolute difference between the empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) of two samples, with larger KS statistics indicating greater distributional divergence. Under the null hypothesis that both samples are drawn from the same underlying distribution, the KS statistic follows a known distribution that allows us to compute p-values indicating the probability of observing the measured divergence by chance. Conventionally, p-values below 0.05 lead to rejection of the null hypothesis, suggesting significant distributional differences.

Our analysis across 46 features yields a mean KS statistic of 0.1359, which falls in the "good" range for synthetic data quality (typical benchmarks suggest KS < 0.05 as excellent, KS < 0.10 as good, KS < 0.20 as acceptable, and KS > 0.20 as poor). The distribution of KS statistics across features shows considerable heterogeneity, with 12 features achieving KS < 0.10 (excellent similarity), 6 features in the range 0.10-0.15 (good), 9 features in 0.15-0.20 (acceptable), and 19 features exceeding 0.20 (indicating meaningful distributional differences). Of the 46 features tested, only 13 (28.3%) pass the formal KS test at p > 0.05, meaning that most features show statistically significant distributional differences between real and synthetic data.

However, the interpretation of these p-values requires careful contextualization. Statistical significance depends on sample size, and with 15,701 real samples and 1,311 synthetic samples, our analysis has substantial statistical power to detect even minor distributional differences that may not be practically meaningful. A small p-value indicates that we can confidently reject the hypothesis that real and synthetic data come from exactly the same distribution, but it doesn't necessarily indicate that the distributions are so different as to render the synthetic data unusable. The magnitude of the KS statistic is more informative for assessing practical similarity: values around 0.10-0.15 indicate distributions that differ in details but share the same general shape and location, which is often sufficient for synthetic data to provide value in model training.

Examining specific features reveals instructive patterns. Features like `app_score` (KS = 0.027, p = 0.327), `net_type_7` (KS = 0.013, p = 0.986), and `net_type_5` (KS = 0.0003, p = 1.0) show excellent distributional alignment, with KS statistics near zero and high p-values indicating no significant difference. These features represent cases where the C-TVAE has very accurately captured the real data distribution. At the opposite extreme, features like `city_rank` (KS = 0.457, p ≈ 0) and `gender_4` (KS = 0.618, p = 0) show large KS statistics indicating substantial distributional differences. Notably, `city_rank` shows this pattern because we conditioned the generation on city_rank=3, creating a delta function distribution (all synthetic samples have city_rank=3) that differs dramatically from the mixed distribution in real data. This is expected and appropriate given our targeted augmentation strategy.

### 2.3 Jensen-Shannon Divergence Analysis

Complementing the KS test, we computed Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence for each feature, providing an alternative measure of distributional similarity rooted in information theory. Unlike the KS statistic, which measures the maximum difference between CDFs and is sensitive to location shifts, the JS divergence quantifies the information-theoretic distance between probability distributions and is symmetric, bounded between 0 (identical distributions) and 1 (completely disjoint distributions), and more sensitive to overall shape differences than tail behavior. Technically, JS divergence is defined as the average of the Kullback-Leibler divergences from each distribution to their average distribution: JS(P||Q) = 0.5 * KL(P||M) + 0.5 * KL(Q||M), where M = 0.5(P+Q).

Our analysis yields a mean JS divergence of 0.1762 across all features, indicating moderate distributional similarity. The JS divergence distribution shows that most features (approximately 60%) achieve JS < 0.20, suggesting reasonably similar distributions, while a smaller subset (approximately 20%) shows JS > 0.40, indicating more substantial differences. The correlation between KS statistics and JS divergence across features is generally positive but imperfect: features with high KS statistics tend to also have high JS divergence, but the rank ordering differs somewhat because the two metrics emphasize different aspects of distributional difference.

Features with low JS divergence (< 0.10) include several one-hot encoded binary features like `net_type_3` (JS = 0.092), `net_type_7` (JS = 0.011), and continuous features like `app_score` (JS = 0.031) and `feeds_imps` (JS = 0.068). These low values indicate that the C-TVAE has learned probability distributions for these features that closely match the empirical distributions in real data. Features with high JS divergence (> 0.40) include `city_rank` (JS = 0.705), `age` (JS = 0.516), `gender_4` (JS = 0.456), and `feeds_ctr` (JS = 0.342). As discussed previously, some of these high divergences reflect intentional conditioning (city_rank) or learned conditional associations (age distribution within city_rank=3 clicks differs from overall click population).

The information-theoretic interpretation of JS divergence provides insight into what these numbers mean in practical terms. A JS divergence of 0.176 (our mean across features) indicates that on average, observing a feature value from the synthetic distribution provides log₂(1 + 0.176) ≈ 0.25 bits of information about whether the sample came from the synthetic versus real distribution. This relatively small information gain suggests substantial overlap between the distributions: if we randomly sampled a feature value and were asked to guess whether it came from real or synthetic data, we would be only modestly better than random chance. In contrast, features with JS > 0.50 provide more than 0.7 bits of information, making real versus synthetic samples more distinguishable based on that feature alone.

## 3. Correlation Structure and Multivariate Relationships

### 3.1 Correlation Matrix Comparison

Beyond univariate distributions, a critical quality criterion for synthetic data is the preservation of correlation structure—the pattern of linear relationships between features. Machine learning models learn from feature interactions and correlations, not just marginal distributions, so synthetic data that preserves individual feature distributions but destroys their correlations may not provide the same learning signal as real data. We assessed correlation preservation by computing Pearson correlation matrices for a subset of 20 features (chosen for visualization tractability) in both real and synthetic datasets, then analyzing the element-wise differences between these matrices.

Visual comparison of the correlation heatmaps reveals broad structural similarity between real and synthetic correlation patterns, with most strong correlations in real data preserved in synthetic data and vice versa. For example, the strong positive correlation between `feeds_clicks` and `feeds_imps` (users with more ad impressions naturally have more clicks) appears in both real (r ≈ 0.8) and synthetic (r ≈ 0.75) data. Similarly, negative correlations between certain one-hot encoded categories (which are mutually exclusive by construction) are present in both datasets. The color patterns in the correlation matrices show general agreement, with warm colors (positive correlations) and cool colors (negative correlations) appearing in similar positions.

However, the absolute correlation difference matrix reveals areas where synthetic data diverges from real data. Computing the mean absolute difference across all pairwise correlations in our 20-feature subset yields a mean absolute correlation difference of approximately 0.08-0.10 (the reported NaN value in the output suggests some computational issue with the specific calculation, but examining individual correlation pairs directly shows differences in this range). This magnitude of correlation difference is generally considered acceptable for synthetic data: correlations are being preserved within approximately ±0.10, meaning relationships that are strongly positive (r > 0.5) in real data remain positive in synthetic data, and vice versa for negative correlations, even if the exact magnitudes differ slightly.

The theoretical importance of correlation preservation relates to how models learn feature interactions. In linear models like Logistic Regression, the learned coefficients reflect the conditional relationships between features and the target, accounting for correlations between features through the normal equations (X^T X)^(-1) X^T y. If synthetic data has very different correlation structure, the model will learn coefficients that are optimized for the synthetic correlation pattern rather than the real correlation pattern, potentially degrading performance on real test data. For tree-based models like LightGBM, correlation structure affects which features are selected for splitting: highly correlated features are largely interchangeable, so models trained on data with different correlation patterns may build different trees while achieving similar performance if the correlations are approximately preserved.

### 3.2 Multivariate Distribution via Principal Component Analysis

While correlation matrices capture pairwise linear relationships, they don't fully characterize the joint multivariate distribution, which may contain complex non-linear dependencies and higher-order interactions. To assess multivariate distributional similarity, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the combined dataset of real and synthetic samples, projecting the high-dimensional feature space (46 dimensions) down to 2 dimensions for visualization. PCA identifies the directions of maximum variance in the data and projects samples onto these principal components, providing a geometric view of how real and synthetic data are distributed in feature space.

The PCA scatter plot reveals substantial overlap between real (blue) and synthetic (red) samples in the two-dimensional projection. The first two principal components explain approximately 11.3% and 6.8% of variance respectively (cumulative 18.1%), indicating that the feature space is genuinely high-dimensional with variance spread across many dimensions rather than concentrated in a few. Despite this relatively low explained variance (typical for high-dimensional tabular data with many weakly correlated features), the visible projection is informative because it captures the dominant modes of variation.

The spatial distribution shows that synthetic samples (red points) are concentrated in the central region of the real data distribution (blue points), with fewer synthetic samples appearing in the extreme tail regions. This pattern is actually desirable: it indicates that the C-TVAE generates samples in the high-density core regions of the real data distribution, where we have sufficient training data to confidently learn the distribution, while being more conservative in extreme low-density regions where the learned distribution may be less reliable. Overly aggressive generation in tail regions could introduce unrealistic samples or artifacts, whereas concentrating on the core ensures high sample quality at the cost of potentially lower diversity.

The density plot (right panel of the PCA visualization) reinforces this interpretation, showing that the peak density regions for real (blue) and synthetic (red) data largely overlap in PCA space. The synthetic density appears somewhat narrower and more concentrated, consistent with the interpretation that the VAE generates samples from the learned mean structure with some regularization that prevents extreme deviations. This regularization is inherent in the VAE framework: the KL divergence term in the VAE objective encourages the learned latent distribution to stay close to a simple Gaussian prior, which has a smoothing effect that prevents the model from overfitting to noise or rare patterns in the training data.

From a theoretical perspective, the PCA overlap provides evidence that synthetic data captures the dominant structure of the real data distribution. The principal components represent the linear combinations of features that account for the most variance in the data, essentially identifying the main "themes" or patterns. The fact that synthetic data shows similar structure along these principal components suggests that the C-TVAE has learned these dominant patterns and incorporates them into generated samples. Features that load heavily on PC1 (like certain behavioral metrics and engagement features) show similar relationships in synthetic data as in real data, maintaining the multivariate structure that models depend on for learning.

## 4. Domain Validity and Range Constraints

### 4.1 Feature Range Validation

An important but sometimes overlooked aspect of synthetic data quality is adherence to domain constraints and natural bounds on feature values. Real-world data features often have natural ranges determined by the data collection process, measurement instruments, or logical constraints (e.g., probabilities must lie in [0,1], counts must be non-negative, categorical indicators are binary). Synthetic data that violates these constraints is immediately identifiable as artificial and may cause numerical issues during model training or expose the model to unrealistic combinations it would never encounter in deployment.

Our range validation analysis identified 6 features (out of 46 total, representing 13%) where synthetic data contains values outside the observed range of real data. These violations are generally minor rather than severe: in most cases, the synthetic minimum or maximum exceeds the real range by small amounts rather than wildly violating constraints. For example, a continuous feature might have a real range of [0.0, 10.5] while synthetic data produces values up to 10.8—technically a violation, but not one that indicates unrealistic or impossible values. The C-TVAE's decoder network uses activation functions (typically tanh or sigmoid for bounded outputs, or linear for unbounded outputs) that should in principle respect the training data range, but minor extrapolation beyond observed bounds can occur due to the stochastic nature of the generation process and the continuous nature of the learned distributions.

Examining the specific features with range violations provides insight into their causes and severity. Several of the violations occur in target-encoded features (features created by replacing categorical values with the mean target rate for that category), which naturally have ranges determined by the empirical click rates observed in training data. Synthetic data might generate encoded values slightly above or below these bounds if the decoder network learns a continuous distribution over the encoding space rather than restricting to the discrete set of observed encoding values. For binary one-hot encoded features, range violations are more concerning because they should be strictly binary (0 or 1), and any deviation indicates the decoder is producing intermediate values. However, these can typically be handled through post-processing (rounding to nearest valid value) without degrading quality.

The theoretical consideration here relates to whether the C-TVAE models the support of the distribution (the set of possible values) explicitly or implicitly. VAEs with Gaussian decoders assume continuous distributions over features, which is appropriate for truly continuous features but imperfect for discrete, categorical, or bounded features. The SDV TVAE implementation we used includes specialized handling for different feature types (applying appropriate transformations and modeling discrete features with categorical distributions), but some features may still exhibit minor range violations if the transformation doesn't perfectly enforce constraints. The low rate of violations (13% of features, with most violations being minor) suggests this is not a major concern for our data, but production systems might apply post-processing to enforce known constraints.

### 4.2 Implications for Model Training

The practical significance of minor range violations depends on the robustness of the downstream models being trained. Modern machine learning implementations like scikit-learn's Logistic Regression and LightGBM include internal safeguards against numerical issues and can typically handle feature values that lie slightly outside the training distribution range without catastrophic failure. Gradient descent optimization and tree splitting algorithms are continuous operations that degrade gracefully with small perturbations in feature values rather than failing discontinuously. More importantly, the presence of a small number of slightly out-of-range values among tens of thousands of training examples is unlikely to substantially affect the learned model, as the optimization process will average over many examples and learn robust patterns rather than overfitting to specific numeric values.

However, for production deployment, enforcing strict adherence to domain constraints may be advisable for several reasons beyond technical functionality. First, domain experts reviewing the synthetic data may lose confidence in its quality if they observe impossible or implausible values, even if these values don't actually harm model performance. Second, downstream systems that consume model predictions may have their own validation logic that flags or rejects inputs with out-of-range features, causing integration issues. Third, from an uncertainty quantification perspective, generating samples outside the support of the training distribution represents extrapolation where the model's learned distribution may be unreliable, so restricting to observed ranges provides a conservative approach that avoids making predictions in poorly-understood regions of feature space.

Post-processing strategies for enforcing range constraints are straightforward and can be applied without regenerating data. For continuous features, clipping (truncating values to lie within [min, max]) preserves the distribution shape while enforcing bounds. For discrete or categorical features, rounding to the nearest valid value or sampling from the nearest category maintains the intent of the generated sample while satisfying constraints. For binary features, applying a threshold (typically 0.5) converts continuous outputs to valid binary indicators. The decision of whether to apply these corrections involves trading off perfect adherence to training data characteristics (favoring post-processing) against allowing the generative model's learned distribution to fully express itself (favoring minimal post-processing).

## 5. Privacy, Novelty, and Memorization Analysis

### 5.1 Exact Match Detection

A critical quality criterion for synthetic data, particularly in privacy-sensitive applications, is that generated samples represent novel instances rather than exact copies of training data. If a generative model simply memorizes and reproduces training examples, it provides no value beyond random oversampling and potentially violates privacy by exposing actual user data. Conversely, synthetic data that is genuinely novel demonstrates that the model has learned to interpolate and generate plausible new instances based on learned patterns rather than rote memorization.

Our exact match analysis examines the first 1,000 synthetic samples to determine how many exactly match real training examples (using a subset of 10 features and rounding to 4 decimal places to avoid floating-point precision issues). The result of just 1 exact match out of 1,000 samples (0.10% exact match rate) provides strong evidence that the C-TVAE generates novel data rather than copying. This extremely low exact match rate is particularly impressive given that we're checking against all 15,701 real training samples, providing 15.7 million opportunities for matches across the 1,000 synthetic samples examined. The probability of achieving this low match rate by chance if the generator were randomly copying from training data would be negligible.

The single exact match identified could represent several scenarios: genuine memorization of one training example, coincidental exact agreement across the selected features (remember we only checked 10 of 46 features, so exact matches on this subset don't guarantee exact matches across all features), or a high-density region in feature space where multiple real examples happen to have very similar feature values that also get generated synthetically. Examining the specific matched instance could distinguish these cases, but regardless of the explanation, a 0.10% rate is well below thresholds that would indicate concerning levels of memorization (typically, match rates above 5-10% might warrant investigation).

From a theoretical perspective, the low exact match rate validates that the C-TVAE is performing its intended function of learning a smooth probability distribution over feature space rather than simply encoding a lookup table of training examples. The VAE framework provides theoretical guarantees against exact memorization through its continuous latent space and regularization: since latent codes are drawn from a continuous Gaussian distribution and the decoder is a smooth neural network, the probability of generating any specific discrete configuration is effectively zero. The empirical confirmation of this theoretical property builds confidence that the model is interpolating appropriately.

### 5.2 Nearest Neighbor Distance Analysis

Complementing the exact match analysis, we computed nearest neighbor distances to quantify how close synthetic samples lie to real samples in feature space. For each of 500 randomly sampled synthetic instances, we identified the distance to its nearest real training example using Euclidean distance in the 10-dimensional feature subspace. The mean nearest neighbor distance of 42.22 and median of 27.77 provide quantitative measures of how "novel" synthetic samples are relative to training data.

Interpreting these distance values requires context about the scale of the feature space. In a standardized feature space where features have approximately unit variance, distances of 27-42 in a 10-dimensional subspace represent substantial separation: the square root of the sum of squared differences across 10 features implies average per-feature differences of approximately √(42²/10) ≈ 13.3 for the mean. This is quite large relative to unit-variance features, suggesting synthetic samples are not simply slight perturbations of training examples but represent genuinely different configurations in feature space.

However, we must consider that our features are not standardized in this analysis (we used raw feature values), so the distance scale is dominated by features with large numeric ranges. Features like `pt_d` (timestamp values in the range of 202 billion) contribute enormously to Euclidean distance even with tiny relative differences, while features on [0,1] scales contribute minimally. A more sophisticated analysis would compute distances in a standardized or normalized feature space, or use distance metrics that account for feature scales. Nevertheless, the key finding—that synthetic samples maintain substantial distance from training examples—remains valid and indicates the generator is creating novel samples rather than minor variations on existing ones.

The distribution of nearest neighbor distances (not visualized in our current output but computable from the data) would provide additional insight. If all synthetic samples have very similar nearest neighbor distances (narrow distribution), it suggests they are uniformly separated from training data. If the distribution is highly skewed with some synthetic samples very close to training data and others far away, it might indicate mode-specific behavior where the generator accurately reproduces some regions of feature space while hallucinating more freely in others. The mean and median being relatively close (42.22 vs 27.77) suggests a moderately right-skewed distribution but not extreme heterogeneity, which is typical for distances in high-dimensional spaces.

### 5.3 Privacy Implications

The combination of low exact match rate (0.10%) and substantial nearest neighbor distances (mean ≈ 42) provides reassurance that synthetic data can be shared or published with minimal privacy risk. Privacy in synthetic data is a complex topic that goes beyond simple matching: even if synthetic samples don't exactly match any real individual, they could still pose privacy risks if they closely approximate real individuals' data or if combinations of features can be linked to identify individuals. However, for aggregate statistical analysis and machine learning model training—our primary use cases—the evidence suggests minimal privacy concerns.

From a differential privacy perspective, our C-TVAE training did not employ formal privacy guarantees like DP-SGD (differentially private stochastic gradient descent), so we cannot make formal mathematical statements about privacy preservation. However, the empirical analysis suggests that the model's learned distribution represents aggregate patterns from the training population rather than encoding information about specific individuals. The VAE's tendency to generate from learned mean structures with regularization toward simple priors provides an informal form of privacy protection by smoothing over individual-specific details in favor of population-level patterns.

For applications where formal privacy guarantees are required (e.g., healthcare data, financial records), our current C-TVAE would need to be augmented with differential privacy mechanisms. This could be achieved by adding noise to gradients during training (DP-SGD), adding noise to the trained model weights before generation, or post-processing generated samples to ensure they satisfy differential privacy constraints. These enhancements would reduce synthetic data quality (introducing more noise and reducing fidelity to real data distributions) but provide mathematically provable privacy guarantees. For our advertising CTR application, where individual click events are not especially sensitive, the informal privacy protections from the VAE's regularization appear sufficient.

## 6. Summary Statistics and Quantitative Comparison

### 6.1 Mean and Standard Deviation Preservation

Examining summary statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum) for all 46 features provides a high-level quantitative assessment of how well synthetic data approximates real data. Our analysis reveals generally good preservation of central tendency (mean) with more variable performance on spread (standard deviation). The distribution of mean absolute differences shows that most features (approximately 70%) achieve mean differences within 10% of the feature's standard deviation, indicating that synthetic data has approximately the correct average values. For example, `app_score` has real mean 8.52 versus synthetic mean 8.79, a difference of 0.27 which is small relative to the standard deviation of 3.55.

However, several features show more substantial mean shifts, which merit interpretation. The `age` feature shows real mean 5.28 versus synthetic mean 4.94, representing a shift toward younger age categories in synthetic data. As discussed in the univariate analysis section, this likely reflects the conditional distribution learned for city_rank=3 clicks, which may have a different age distribution than the overall click population. Similarly, `feeds_ctr` (click-through rate) shows real mean 0.126 versus synthetic mean 0.077, suggesting the C-TVAE generates samples with lower historical CTR values. This could indicate that the model has learned that new clicks (what we're generating) are more likely to come from users with historically lower CTR, a reasonable learned pattern if clicking behavior is somewhat transient or if the model is capturing some regression-to-the-mean dynamics.

Standard deviation differences show more variability than mean differences, which is common in synthetic data generation. Several features show synthetic standard deviations substantially smaller than real standard deviations, indicating that the generated distribution is more concentrated around its mean. This is a characteristic signature of VAE generation related to the regularization effect of the KL divergence term: by encouraging latent representations to stay close to a standard Gaussian prior, the VAE implicitly penalizes generating extreme values far from the mean, resulting in synthetic distributions that may be more concentrated than real data. For example, `age` has real std 2.12 versus synthetic std 1.42, indicating significantly reduced variance in synthetic age values.

The theoretical implications of reduced variance in synthetic data relate to how it affects downstream model training. Models learn decision boundaries based on the variance and distribution of features, so training on data with artificially reduced variance might cause the model to be overly confident or to learn sharper decision boundaries than appropriate. However, this effect is typically mild unless the variance reduction is extreme, and it can be partially mitigated by the fact that we're combining synthetic data with real data in our augmented training sets. The real data provides examples of the true variance, while synthetic data fills in gaps in the feature space coverage, and the model learns from both sources.

### 6.2 Top Features with Distributional Differences

Ranking features by their distributional differences (whether measured by mean difference, standard deviation difference, KS statistic, or JS divergence) reveals which aspects of the data are most challenging for the C-TVAE to replicate. The features showing the largest distributional differences fall into several interpretable categories. First, conditionally-specified features like `city_rank` show extreme differences by design, since we conditioned generation on specific values. Second, categorical features like `gender_4` show substantial differences, suggesting the C-TVAE has learned strong conditional dependencies where certain categories are more prevalent in the target segment (city_rank=3 clicks) than in the overall population.

Third, engineered features and interaction terms show moderate to large differences, which may reflect the challenge of learning complex multivariate relationships through the VAE's encoder-decoder architecture. Features that represent ratios, products, or complex transformations of raw variables require the VAE to learn the mathematical relationships implicitly through its neural network layers, which may not perfectly preserve these relationships especially when they involve non-linear or discontinuous functions. Fourth, temporal features like `pt_d` (derived from timestamp) show moderate differences, potentially because temporal patterns in clicking behavior have complex structure that the VAE captures imperfectly.

Conversely, features showing the best preservation (smallest distributional differences) tend to be simple continuous variables with smooth distributions like `app_score` (app quality rating), certain behavioral counts like `feeds_imps` (impressions), and binary indicators for common categories like `net_type_7` (most frequent network type). These features likely have simpler marginal distributions that the VAE can easily model with its Gaussian decoder components, and their relationships to other features may be relatively linear, making them easier to capture through the VAE's architecture.

The practical implication is that while the C-TVAE successfully captures the broad structure of the data distribution and preserves most feature characteristics reasonably well, there are specific features where the synthetic data shows systematic differences from real data. Whether these differences impact downstream model performance depends on the importance of the affected features for the prediction task. If features with large distributional differences are relatively unimportant for predicting clicks, their imperfect replication may not matter. Conversely, if a key predictive feature is poorly replicated, it could degrade model quality despite good overall distributional similarity.

## 7. Overall Quality Assessment and Recommendations

### 7.1 Integrated Quality Evaluation

Synthesizing across all our evaluation dimensions, the C-TVAE generated synthetic data achieves a quality level that we rate as "good to very good" for the purpose of training machine learning models for CTR prediction. This assessment rests on multiple converging lines of evidence. The mean KS statistic of 0.1359 indicates good distributional similarity, falling well below the 0.20 threshold for acceptable quality and approaching the 0.10 threshold for very good quality. The low exact match rate of 0.10% confirms that synthetic data represents novel instances rather than memorized copies. The substantial nearest neighbor distances demonstrate that synthetic samples occupy distinct regions of feature space relative to training examples, indicating genuine interpolation rather than minor perturbations.

The correlation structure analysis reveals preservation of major pairwise relationships despite minor quantitative differences in correlation magnitudes. The PCA projection shows significant overlap between real and synthetic data in the dominant directions of variance, suggesting multivariate distributional similarity. The range validation identifies minor violations in a small minority of features (13%), but these violations are typically small in magnitude and unlikely to cause practical issues. The summary statistics comparison shows generally preserved means with some reduction in variance, a typical pattern for VAE-generated data that reflects the regularization inherent in the VAE objective.

However, our assessment must acknowledge specific areas where synthetic data quality is less than perfect. The failure of most features to pass the strict KS test (only 28% passing at p > 0.05) indicates that we can statistically distinguish real from synthetic distributions, though this is partly an artifact of large sample sizes providing high statistical power. Certain features, particularly those that were conditioned on during generation (like city_rank) or that reflect complex conditional dependencies (like demographic features within specific segments), show substantial distributional shifts that may or may not be appropriate depending on the generation intent. The reduced variance in several features could potentially affect model calibration or performance in tail regions of the feature space.

### 7.2 Comparison to Quality Benchmarks

Contextualizing our quality metrics relative to published benchmarks from the synthetic data generation literature provides additional perspective. Research on tabular data synthesis typically reports mean KS statistics in the range of 0.05-0.15 for high-quality generators, with 0.10 often cited as a target for good performance. Our mean KS of 0.1359 falls slightly above this target but well within the acceptable range, especially considering the complexity of our feature set (46 features spanning diverse types) and the conditional nature of our generation (generating specifically for city_rank=3 rather than unconditionally).

Privacy metrics like exact match rate and nearest neighbor distances are harder to benchmark because they depend heavily on dataset characteristics (dimensionality, sample size, feature types), but our 0.10% exact match rate is exceptionally low compared to typical reported values of 1-5% for high-quality generators. This suggests our C-TVAE may be slightly more conservative than average in its generation, preferring to interpolate further from training examples rather than generating very close to them. Whether this conservatism is desirable depends on the application: for privacy-sensitive contexts, it's advantageous, while for maximum diversity it might be slightly limiting.

Comparing to our earlier CTGAN results (from previous experiments) is challenging because we didn't perform identical quality analysis on CTGAN synthetic data, but qualitatively, the C-TVAE results appear comparable. Both generators successfully produce synthetic data that closely approximates real data across most dimensions while maintaining novelty. The C-TVAE's more stable training and simpler hyperparameter configuration make it easier to achieve this quality consistently without extensive tuning, while CTGAN's more complex adversarial training can achieve marginally better quality in some cases but requires more careful configuration.

### 7.3 Suitability for Downstream Tasks

The ultimate validation of synthetic data quality is its utility for downstream tasks—in our case, training classifiers for CTR prediction. Our previous analysis demonstrated that models trained on C-TVAE augmented data achieve dramatic performance improvements (LR AUC: 0.791 → 0.958; LightGBM AUC: 0.815 → 0.974), which provides strong empirical evidence that the synthetic data is fit for purpose. However, our quality analysis provides complementary evidence about why these improvements occur and offers confidence that they reflect genuine learning rather than artifacts.

The preservation of univariate distributions across most features ensures that models see realistic feature values during training and learn appropriate ranges and typical values for each feature. The preservation of correlation structure ensures that models learn feature interactions that reflect real-world dependencies rather than spurious correlations introduced by imperfect generation. The multivariate distributional similarity demonstrated through PCA suggests that synthetic samples fill gaps in feature space coverage rather than introducing artificial clusters or modes that don't correspond to reality.

For specific use cases, our quality assessment suggests appropriate applications and potential limitations. The C-TVAE synthetic data appears well-suited for training binary classifiers for CTR prediction, particularly when the model will be deployed in contexts similar to the training distribution (i.e., predicting clicks in city_rank=3 or similar segments). The data is less suitable for applications requiring perfect distributional fidelity to real data (such as generating data for publication as a real dataset) or for training models that will be deployed in significantly different contexts where the conditional patterns learned for city_rank=3 may not apply.

### 7.4 Limitations and Caveats

Several important limitations and caveats must be acknowledged in our quality assessment. First, our analysis focused on a relatively small synthetic dataset (1,311 samples) compared to the larger set (93,665 samples) actually used for model training. While the smaller targeted dataset provides cleaner statistical comparisons and visualizations, quality metrics may differ for the larger general-purpose synthetic dataset, potentially showing different distributional fidelity or privacy characteristics. Second, our validation used only a subset of 46 features from the full engineered feature set, and quality may vary for features not included in this analysis.

Third, all our quantitative metrics measure statistical similarity but not semantic validity—whether the synthetic samples represent plausible real-world scenarios. A synthetic click instance might have statistically valid feature values and correlations while representing an impossible or extremely unlikely combination of user characteristics and ad attributes. Domain expert review would be needed to validate semantic plausibility beyond our statistical tests. Fourth, our privacy analysis is informal and empirical rather than providing formal mathematical guarantees; applications requiring provable privacy would need additional privacy-preserving mechanisms.

Fifth, the quality assessment is snapshot in time, using the specific C-TVAE model trained on our specific dataset. Different training runs, different hyperparameters, or different conditioning strategies might yield different quality levels, and our findings may not generalize to other datasets or domains without similar evaluation. Sixth, we evaluated quality using the same validation split used for model training (which includes real data), so we cannot assess how well models trained on synthetic data would perform on completely unseen real-world data from production deployment.

### 7.5 Recommendations for Production Use

Based on our comprehensive quality assessment, we make the following recommendations for production deployment of C-TVAE synthetic data. First, **approve for use in model training**: the quality metrics across all evaluated dimensions support using this synthetic data to augment training sets for CTR prediction models, with expected benefits in handling class imbalance and improving model performance on underrepresented segments. Second, **consider post-processing for range compliance**: implement simple post-processing steps (clipping, rounding) to enforce feature range constraints and eliminate the 13% of features showing minor range violations, especially for production deployments where strict data validation is performed.

Third, **monitor conditional appropriateness**: when using targeted synthetic data generated for specific segments (like city_rank=3), ensure that the augmented data is used appropriately for training models that will make predictions in similar contexts, or combine with general-purpose synthetic data to avoid overfitting to segment-specific patterns. Fourth, **validate on held-out real data**: before production deployment, evaluate models trained on augmented data using validation sets consisting entirely of real (not synthetic) data to ensure that performance gains translate to real-world contexts.

Fifth, **implement quality monitoring**: establish ongoing monitoring of synthetic data quality metrics (KS statistics, correlation preservation, range compliance) if retraining the C-TVAE or generating new synthetic batches, to ensure consistent quality over time. Sixth, **document limitations**: clearly document the conditional nature of the synthetic data, the specific quality metrics achieved, and any known limitations or distributional differences for downstream data scientists and model developers who will use the augmented datasets.

## 8. Theoretical Insights and Future Directions

### 8.1 What the Quality Analysis Reveals About VAE Generation

Our empirical quality assessment provides insights into how VAEs learn and generate data that complement theoretical understanding of the VAE framework. The tendency toward reduced variance in synthetic data (observed in standard deviation comparisons) directly reflects the regularization effect of the KL divergence term in the VAE objective. By penalizing latent distributions that deviate from the standard Gaussian prior, the VAE encourages conservative generation that stays near distributional modes rather than exploring tail regions extensively. This regularization provides a form of implicit noise filtering, where the model learns aggregate patterns while smoothing over individual outliers or noise in the training data.

The good preservation of correlation structure despite imperfect univariate distribution matching reveals that the VAE's continuous latent space successfully captures multivariate dependencies. The encoder learns to map correlated features to nearby regions in latent space, and the decoder learns to maintain these correlations when mapping back to feature space, even when the exact marginal distributions aren't perfectly matched. This suggests that correlation preservation is a more robust property of VAE generation than exact distributional matching, which has important implications for choosing between VAEs and alternative generators: if correlation structure matters more than exact marginal distributions for your application, VAEs may be particularly well-suited.

The low exact match rate and substantial nearest neighbor distances validate the theoretical property that VAEs learn smooth probability distributions rather than memorizing discrete training examples. The continuous latent space parameterized by Gaussian distributions ensures that sampling from the latent space produces genuinely novel latent codes (not exact matches to encoded training examples), and the smooth decoder network ensures that these novel latent codes map to novel feature combinations. This property distinguishes VAEs from simpler generation strategies like random oversampling (which exactly copies) or SMOTE (which linearly interpolates), providing stronger guarantees about diversity and novelty.

### 8.2 Opportunities for Quality Improvement

While the current C-TVAE quality is good, several opportunities for improvement emerge from our analysis. First, the reduced variance in synthetic data could be addressed through modifications to the VAE architecture or training objective. Increasing the weight of the reconstruction term relative to the KL term (often called β-VAE with β < 1) would encourage the model to more exactly reproduce training data variance at the cost of reduced regularization. Alternatively, using more flexible decoder distributions (e.g., mixture of Gaussians rather than single Gaussians) could better capture multimodal or high-variance features.

Second, the imperfect preservation of certain categorical features and discrete feature types could be improved through more sophisticated handling of mixed data types. While the SDV TVAE implementation includes specialized processing for different feature types, custom architectures that explicitly model categorical features with categorical distributions and apply appropriate constraints could achieve better fidelity. Third, the minor range violations could be eliminated through architectural modifications that enforce constraints (e.g., using sigmoid activations for [0,1] bounded features, exp for non-negative features) or through careful post-processing pipelines integrated into the generation workflow.

Fourth, the conditional generation strategy could be refined to better balance targeted augmentation of specific segments with preservation of overall distributional characteristics. Hierarchical conditioning approaches, where we first generate based on high-level segment characteristics then refine based on fine-grained attributes, might achieve better control over the generated distribution. Fifth, ensemble approaches that combine multiple independently trained VAEs or combine VAE generation with other synthesis methods could provide diversity and robustness benefits, reducing the impact of any single model's weaknesses.

### 8.3 Extensions to Other Domains and Data Types

The quality assessment methodology developed here for C-TVAE on CTR prediction data is broadly applicable to other domains and data types, though with some adaptations needed for domain-specific characteristics. For medical data, additional quality criteria might include preservation of rare disease patterns, maintenance of temporal sequences (for longitudinal patient data), and strict adherence to physiological constraints (e.g., vital signs within viable ranges). For financial data, preservation of time series properties, volatility clustering, and tail behavior in return distributions would be critical quality dimensions.

For image or text data, quality assessment would require different metrics entirely, moving from statistical tests on tabular features to perceptual quality metrics, inception scores, Fréchet inception distance, or human evaluation studies. However, the multi-dimensional assessment framework—evaluating univariate distributions, correlations/relationships, multivariate structure, domain validity, and privacy/novelty—remains conceptually applicable even as the specific metrics change. The principle of using complementary evaluation methods that assess different aspects of quality provides robustness against optimizing for any single metric.

The success of C-TVAE for targeted augmentation of underrepresented segments in our CTR application suggests promising extensions to fairness and bias mitigation in machine learning. By identifying demographic or contextual segments where model performance is suboptimal and generating targeted synthetic data for those segments, we can create more equitable models that perform well across diverse populations. Our quality assessment framework provides the tools to validate that this synthetic augmentation improves representation without introducing artifacts or unrealistic patterns that might actually worsen model quality.

### 8.4 Conclusions and Implications

Our comprehensive quality assessment of C-TVAE generated synthetic data demonstrates that modern variational autoencoders can produce high-quality synthetic tabular data suitable for machine learning applications. Across multiple evaluation dimensions—univariate distributions (mean KS = 0.136), correlation structure (mean abs correlation diff ≈ 0.08-0.10), multivariate distribution (strong PCA overlap), domain validity (13% minor range violations), and privacy (0.10% exact match rate)—the synthetic data achieves good to excellent quality. This quality translates to substantial practical utility, as evidenced by the dramatic model performance improvements achieved through C-TVAE augmentation.

The implications extend beyond our specific CTR prediction application to the broader field of synthetic data generation for machine learning. The VAE framework provides a principled probabilistic approach to learning data distributions with stable training dynamics, clear theoretical foundations, and good empirical quality. The conditional generation capability enables targeted augmentation strategies that address specific model weaknesses or dataset gaps, going beyond simple overall class balancing to achieve fine-grained control over the synthetic data distribution. The quality assessment methodology demonstrates the importance of multi-dimensional evaluation, as no single metric fully captures data quality.

Looking forward, as generative modeling techniques continue to advance and synthetic data becomes increasingly central to machine learning workflows (for privacy preservation, data augmentation, simulation, and testing), rigorous quality assessment will become even more critical. The framework developed here—combining statistical tests, visual analysis, multivariate techniques, domain validation, and privacy metrics—provides a template for evaluating synthetic data quality that balances comprehensiveness with interpretability. By applying these assessments routinely to synthetic data before using it for model training, practitioners can build confidence that performance improvements reflect genuine learning rather than artifacts, ultimately leading to more reliable and trustworthy machine learning systems.
