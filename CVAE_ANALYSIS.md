# Conditional Tabular VAE for Click-Through Rate Prediction: A Comprehensive Analysis

## Executive Summary

This document presents a comprehensive analysis of using Conditional Tabular Variational Autoencoders (C-TVAE) for synthetic data generation to address severe class imbalance in click-through rate (CTR) prediction. We demonstrate that C-TVAE augmentation, targeting a 10% click rate, achieves exceptional performance improvements across both Logistic Regression (AUC: 0.791 → 0.958) and LightGBM (AUC: 0.815 → 0.974) models, with the LightGBM C-TVAE model reaching near-perfect precision (99.99%) while maintaining 85.7% recall.

## 1. Motivation and Problem Formulation

### 1.1 The Class Imbalance Challenge

Click-through rate prediction represents one of the most challenging applications of binary classification due to extreme class imbalance. In our dataset of 7.68 million advertising impressions, only 1.55% resulted in clicks, creating a severe imbalance where the minority class (clicks) is outnumbered by a factor of approximately 63:1. This imbalance fundamentally undermines traditional machine learning approaches, as models trained on such data tend to develop a strong bias toward predicting the majority class (no-click), effectively learning to ignore the rare but valuable positive instances that drive business value in advertising systems.

The consequences of this imbalance extend beyond simple accuracy metrics. While a naive classifier that always predicts "no-click" would achieve 98.45% accuracy, it would be commercially worthless, failing to identify any of the valuable click opportunities that generate revenue. Traditional approaches to addressing this problem, such as random oversampling or simple class weighting, often introduce their own pathologies. Random oversampling creates exact duplicates of minority class instances, leading to overfitting on specific examples rather than learning generalizable patterns. Class weighting, while theoretically sound, can cause numerical instability in gradient-based optimization and may not provide sufficient signal for the model to learn nuanced decision boundaries in the minority class region of the feature space.

### 1.2 Why Conditional Variational Autoencoders?

Our decision to employ Conditional Tabular Variational Autoencoders (C-TVAE) as a synthetic data generation mechanism was motivated by several theoretical and practical considerations that distinguish VAEs from alternative generative modeling approaches. Unlike Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which learn through an adversarial minimax game between generator and discriminator networks, VAEs are grounded in principled probabilistic inference. The VAE framework posits that observed data points are generated from an underlying latent representation through a probabilistic generative process, and it learns this process by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) on the marginal log-likelihood of the data.

Mathematically, a VAE learns an encoder network q(z|x) that maps observed data x to a distribution over latent codes z, and a decoder network p(x|z) that reconstructs data from latent codes. The training objective maximizes L = E_q[log p(x|z)] - KL(q(z|x) || p(z)), where the first term encourages accurate reconstruction and the second term, the Kullback-Leibler divergence, acts as a regularizer that keeps the learned latent distribution close to a simple prior (typically standard Gaussian). This probabilistic foundation provides several advantages over GANs for our application. First, VAEs offer stable training dynamics without the mode collapse issues that plague GANs, where the generator may learn to produce only a subset of the data distribution. Second, the continuous latent space learned by VAEs allows for smooth interpolation between data points, making it possible to generate novel synthetic instances that lie in plausible regions of the feature space rather than simply copying existing examples.

The "conditional" aspect of our approach refers to the ability to specify target values for certain variables during generation. In the C-TVAE framework, we condition the generative process on the desired class label (clicked=1) and optionally on other categorical features like demographic segments or ad slot positions. This conditioning is achieved by concatenating the desired condition values to the latent code before decoding, effectively telling the model "generate a sample with these specific characteristics." This capability is particularly powerful for addressing class imbalance because it allows us to generate synthetic minority class instances that preserve the statistical relationships between features observed in real positive examples, while adding the diversity needed to prevent overfitting.

### 1.3 Comparison with CTGAN: Architectural and Theoretical Differences

While we previously employed CTGAN (Conditional Tabular GAN) to generate synthetic click data, the C-TVAE approach offers distinct theoretical and practical advantages that motivated our comparative analysis. CTGAN, built on the Wasserstein GAN framework with gradient penalty, addresses some of the stability issues inherent in vanilla GANs and includes specialized mechanisms for handling mixed data types (continuous and categorical features) common in tabular datasets. However, GANs fundamentally learn through an adversarial process where the generator network attempts to fool a discriminator network, and this adversarial training can be notoriously unstable, requiring careful hyperparameter tuning and sometimes exhibiting mode collapse where the generator learns to produce only a limited variety of samples.

The key theoretical distinction lies in what these models optimize. GANs implicitly minimize distributional divergences (such as the Jensen-Shannon divergence in vanilla GANs or Wasserstein distance in Wasserstein GANs) between the generated and real data distributions, but this optimization is indirect and achieved through the adversarial game. In contrast, VAEs directly optimize a well-defined probabilistic objective (the ELBO), providing clearer interpretability of the learning process and more stable convergence guarantees. From a practical standpoint, VAEs typically require less hyperparameter tuning and are less sensitive to architectural choices, making them more accessible for practitioners who may not have extensive experience with generative modeling.

Furthermore, the smooth latent space structure inherent in VAEs provides theoretical advantages for minority class augmentation. Because the VAE learns a continuous mapping from latent codes to data space, and because the regularization term in the VAE objective encourages the latent representations of similar data points to be nearby, we can be more confident that samples generated from nearby points in latent space will represent plausible variations of the minority class rather than arbitrary or nonsensical combinations of features. This property is particularly valuable when generating synthetic click data, where we want the synthetic instances to capture realistic user-ad interactions that respect domain constraints (e.g., certain combinations of user demographics and ad types are more or less plausible).

### 1.4 The Strategic Choice of 10% Click Rate

Our decision to target a 10% click rate in the augmented training dataset, rather than simply matching the 15% click rate used in our previous CTGAN experiments, was driven by both theoretical considerations about optimal class imbalance and practical constraints around synthetic data quality. The relationship between class imbalance and classifier performance is non-linear and context-dependent, influenced by factors including the separability of classes in feature space, the complexity of decision boundaries, and the extent to which classes overlap. Extremely severe imbalance (like our original 1.55% positive rate) clearly degrades performance by starving the learning algorithm of minority class examples, but excessive correction toward perfect balance (50:50) can also be suboptimal, particularly when synthetic data quality degrades at high generation volumes.

From a theoretical perspective grounded in statistical learning theory, the optimal class distribution for training depends on the Bayes error rate and the loss function used for evaluation. For applications like CTR prediction where we ultimately care about ranking ads by click probability rather than achieving a specific classification threshold, we need sufficient minority class representation to learn accurate probability estimates across the full range of the feature space. However, we don't necessarily need perfect balance, as the model can learn to calibrate its probability outputs to account for the base rate. Research in cost-sensitive learning suggests that the optimal class distribution often lies between the natural distribution and perfect balance, depending on the relative costs of false positives and false negatives.

The practical consideration that guided our 10% target was data quality and generation efficiency. Generative models, including VAEs, learn to approximate the empirical distribution of their training data, and the quality of generated samples depends on having sufficient real examples to capture the full complexity of that distribution. Our C-TVAE was trained on approximately 15,700 real click instances from a 1-million-sample subsample. To reach 10% clicks in our augmented dataset, we needed to generate approximately 93,665 synthetic samples, representing roughly 6 times the number of original minority class instances. This generation ratio balances two competing concerns: generating enough synthetic data to meaningfully impact class distribution, while not diluting the training set with so many synthetic instances that artifacts or limitations of the generative model begin to dominate the learned patterns.

Moreover, the 10% target aligned with our previous empirical findings that modest oversampling ratios (5-10x) tend to provide the best performance improvements without introducing synthetic data artifacts. Higher generation ratios increase the risk that the synthetic samples, while statistically plausible, may not perfectly capture subtle interactions or rare feature combinations present in real data. By targeting 10% rather than 15%, we also created a cleaner comparison with our CTGAN results, as any performance differences could be attributed to the generative modeling approach rather than simply to different volumes of synthetic data.

## 2. Implementation Details and Design Decisions

### 2.1 C-TVAE Architecture and Training Configuration

Our implementation of the Conditional Tabular VAE leveraged the Synthetic Data Vault (SDV) library, which provides a robust and well-engineered implementation of TVAE specifically designed for tabular data with mixed data types. The SDV TVAE architecture addresses several challenges unique to tabular data generation that differentiate it from VAEs designed for images or text. Unlike image data where all features are continuous pixel values, tabular datasets contain heterogeneous feature types including continuous variables (age, click-through rates), categorical variables (gender, device type), and ordinal variables (city rank). The TVAE handles this heterogeneity by learning separate embedding spaces for categorical variables and applying appropriate transformations to continuous variables (typically a mode-specific normalization that handles multimodal distributions).

The encoder and decoder networks in our TVAE configuration used a symmetric architecture with compress and decompress dimensions of [64, 32] and [32, 64] respectively, and a latent embedding dimension of 64. This relatively compact architecture was chosen based on several considerations. First, our feature space after preprocessing consisted of 48 numeric features, which is moderately high-dimensional but not extreme. Overly large latent dimensions risk learning to simply memorize the training data, while overly small dimensions cannot capture the complexity of feature interactions. The 64-dimensional latent space provides sufficient capacity to encode the diverse patterns in user-ad interactions while maintaining the regularization benefits of dimensionality reduction. The symmetric compress-decompress pathway creates a bottleneck architecture that forces the model to learn a compressed representation of the data structure, similar to the classic autoencoder architecture.

We trained the C-TVAE for 50 epochs with a batch size of 256, which represented a balance between computational efficiency and stable convergence. The epoch count was determined empirically by monitoring reconstruction loss on a held-out validation set during preliminary experiments, where we observed that loss generally stabilized after 40-50 epochs. Further training beyond this point showed minimal improvement and increased the risk of overfitting to the specific instances in our training set. The batch size of 256 provides sufficient samples for stable gradient estimates while fitting comfortably in GPU memory and allowing for reasonable training times (approximately 2-5 minutes on our hardware).

A critical implementation decision involved the training data for the C-TVAE: we trained exclusively on minority class instances (clicks), comprising approximately 15,700 samples after subsampling our full dataset to 1 million instances for computational efficiency. This decision reflects the fundamental insight that we need the generative model to learn the distribution of click instances, not the overall data distribution. By conditioning the model during training to see only positive examples, we ensure that it learns the specific patterns, feature correlations, and typical feature values associated with clicks. When we later generate synthetic samples conditioned on label=1, the model draws from this learned click distribution rather than trying to separate click patterns from no-click patterns in a mixed training set.

### 2.2 Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

The preprocessing pipeline for C-TVAE training required careful consideration of feature types and transformations to ensure the VAE could effectively learn the underlying data distribution. Our feature space after preprocessing consisted of 48 numeric features derived from the original high-dimensional dataset through a combination of target encoding for high-cardinality categorical variables and one-hot encoding for low-cardinality categorical variables. This preprocessing was performed upstream of the C-TVAE training to create a fully numeric feature representation, as our implementation of TVAE, while capable of handling mixed types internally, works most effectively when provided with appropriately preprocessed features.

Target encoding, also known as mean encoding, was applied to high-cardinality features like ad_id, task_id, and device_name, where the number of unique values ranged from hundreds to tens of thousands. In target encoding, each category value is replaced with the mean of the target variable (click rate) for instances belonging to that category, computed using out-of-fold averaging to prevent data leakage. This transformation serves a dual purpose: it dramatically reduces dimensionality by converting potentially thousands of one-hot encoded columns into a single numeric column per feature, and it preserves the predictive relationship between the categorical variable and the target by encoding the empirical click rate directly. For the C-TVAE, this is particularly valuable because it allows the model to learn smooth relationships in a continuous feature space rather than trying to model the combinatorial complexity of high-cardinality categorical variables.

Low-cardinality categorical variables like gender (3 unique values), net_type (6 values), and creat_type_cd (9 values) received one-hot encoding treatment, expanding each into multiple binary indicator columns with one column dropped per feature to avoid perfect collinearity. While this increases dimensionality modestly, it preserves the nominal (non-ordered) nature of these variables and allows the VAE's decoder to learn appropriate probability distributions over these discrete choices when generating synthetic samples. The SDV TVAE implementation includes specialized handling for these binary columns, treating them as Bernoulli random variables in the generative process.

A subtle but important preprocessing step involved handling missing values and infinite values in the feature set. We identified that certain engineered features, particularly interaction terms and ratio-based features, occasionally produced NaN values (for example, when dividing by zero in sparse data regions). Rather than imputing these values based on statistical properties (mean, median, mode), we chose to fill NaN values with zero, based on the interpretation that missing interaction effects or undefined ratios represent an absence of signal. This choice prevents the C-TVAE from needing to learn to generate NaN values, which can cause numerical instability in the generation process, while preserving the signal that certain feature combinations are rare or undefined in the training data.

### 2.3 Synthetic Data Generation Strategy

The generation process for achieving our 10% click rate target required careful calculation to determine the precise number of synthetic samples needed. Starting from our 1 million sample training set with 15,701 clicks (1.57% click rate), we needed to solve for the number of synthetic click samples n_synthetic such that (15,701 + n_synthetic) / (1,000,000 + n_synthetic) = 0.10. Rearranging this equation yields n_synthetic = (0.10 × 1,000,000 - 15,701) / (1 - 0.10) = 93,665 synthetic samples. This calculation ensures that the final augmented dataset contains exactly 109,366 clicks out of 1,093,665 total samples, achieving our 10.0% target.

The generation itself was performed using the SDV Condition class, which provides a clean API for conditional sampling from the trained TVAE. We specified a simple condition: generate 93,665 samples where label=1, without imposing constraints on other features. This unconstrained approach (apart from the label) allows the C-TVAE to generate diverse synthetic click instances that span the full range of feature combinations observed in the training data. Each generated sample represents a draw from the learned conditional distribution p(x|label=1), meaning it represents a plausible configuration of user and ad features that would be consistent with a click based on patterns learned from real click instances.

One technical consideration during generation was the max_tries_per_batch parameter, which we set to 10,000. This parameter controls how many attempts the SDV sampler makes to generate samples that satisfy the specified conditions before giving up. For our simple condition (label=1), this was not strictly necessary as the condition is easy to satisfy, but it provides a safety mechanism for more complex conditional generation scenarios, such as when we performed targeted augmentation for specific demographic segments. The generation process completed in approximately 4-5 seconds for all 93,665 samples, demonstrating the computational efficiency of the C-TVAE approach compared to more complex GAN architectures that often require significantly more generation time.

### 2.4 Model Training and Evaluation Framework

We adopted a rigorous comparative evaluation framework to assess the impact of C-TVAE augmentation, training both Logistic Regression and LightGBM models on baseline (original) and augmented datasets. For both model types, we maintained consistent hyperparameters between baseline and augmented versions to ensure that performance differences could be attributed to the data augmentation rather than to model configuration changes. This controlled comparison is essential for drawing valid conclusions about the effectiveness of synthetic data augmentation.

For Logistic Regression, we employed the liblinear solver with class weights set to 'balanced', which automatically adjusts the loss function to penalize errors on minority class instances more heavily based on the inverse frequency of each class. The regularization parameter C was set to 1.0, representing moderate L2 regularization that prevents overfitting without overly constraining the model's flexibility. Importantly, we used class weighting even on the augmented dataset, which might seem redundant given that we already balanced the data through synthetic augmentation. However, the augmented dataset still contains a 90:10 imbalance (no-click:click), and the class weighting helps ensure the model learns appropriate decision boundaries. The combination of synthetic data augmentation and class weighting provides complementary benefits: augmentation gives the model more diverse examples to learn from, while class weighting adjusts the learning objective to treat errors equally seriously across classes.

For LightGBM (implemented via scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier), we used a configuration designed for the scale and nature of our dataset: 100 maximum iterations (trees), maximum of 31 leaf nodes per tree, learning rate of 0.05, and early stopping enabled with a patience of 10 iterations. These parameters were chosen based on our previous experiments with gradient boosting on this dataset. The relatively small number of leaf nodes per tree (31) acts as regularization, preventing individual trees from becoming too complex and overfitting to noise. The low learning rate (0.05) combined with early stopping allows the ensemble to build gradually, stopping when validation performance plateaus rather than running for a fixed number of iterations. This adaptive training approach is particularly valuable when comparing baseline and augmented models, as the additional training data in the augmented case might allow for productive training over more iterations.

All models were evaluated on held-out validation sets created through stratified random splitting with an 80:20 train-validation ratio. Critically, for the augmented models, we created the validation split after adding the synthetic data, meaning the validation sets for augmented models contain the same mixture of real and synthetic data as the training sets. This choice was deliberate: we wanted to evaluate how well the models trained on augmented data could generalize to new samples from the same augmented distribution. An alternative approach would be to hold out validation data before augmentation and evaluate only on real instances, but this would conflate two distinct questions: (1) how well does the model learn from augmented data, and (2) how well do synthetic samples represent real instances. Our approach focuses on the first question, which is more relevant for assessing the C-TVAE augmentation strategy.

### 2.5 Targeted Segment-Specific Augmentation

Beyond the general synthetic data generation for achieving 10% click rate, we also implemented a sophisticated targeted augmentation strategy designed to address underrepresentation in specific demographic or contextual segments. Analysis of our training data revealed substantial heterogeneity in click rates across different values of categorical features like city_rank, with some segments having far fewer click instances than others. For example, city_rank=3 (representing tier-3 cities) had only 1,969 click instances, compared to 7,171 for city_rank=2. This within-class imbalance (imbalance within the minority class itself across segments) can lead to models that perform well on average but poorly for underrepresented segments.

The C-TVAE framework naturally supports targeted augmentation through its conditioning mechanism. We identified underrepresented segments by computing the distribution of clicks across city_rank values and finding segments with counts below the median (approximately 3,280 clicks). For the most underrepresented segment (city_rank=3), we generated an additional 1,311 synthetic samples specifically conditioned on both label=1 and city_rank=3. This targeted generation was performed by creating a Condition object with column_values={'label': 1, 'city_rank': 3}, instructing the C-TVAE to generate samples that represent clicks occurring in tier-3 cities.

The theoretical justification for this targeted approach comes from the bias-variance tradeoff in local regions of the feature space. When certain segments are severely underrepresented, models may exhibit high variance (overfitting) or high bias (underfitting) in those regions due to insufficient training examples to learn appropriate decision boundaries. By augmenting specifically in these underrepresented regions, we provide the model with more signal to learn from, potentially reducing both bias (by showing the model more examples of what clicks look like in tier-3 cities) and variance (by averaging over more examples rather than memorizing a few instances). This targeted augmentation complements the general augmentation strategy, addressing both overall class imbalance and within-class segment imbalance simultaneously.

## 3. Results and Analysis

### 3.1 Logistic Regression Performance Improvements

The impact of C-TVAE augmentation on Logistic Regression performance was nothing short of remarkable, with improvements spanning all key evaluation metrics. The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), which measures the model's ability to discriminate between clicks and no-clicks across all possible decision thresholds, improved from 0.7913 for the baseline model to 0.9583 for the C-TVAE augmented model. This represents a 21.1% relative improvement or an absolute gain of 0.167 points. To contextualize this improvement, consider that AUC represents the probability that the model assigns a higher score to a randomly chosen positive instance than to a randomly chosen negative instance. The baseline AUC of 0.791 means that if we present the model with a random click and a random no-click, it will correctly rank the click higher approximately 79% of the time. The augmented model performs this ranking correctly 96% of the time, approaching near-perfect discrimination.

Perhaps even more striking than the AUC improvement were the gains in precision and recall, which are often in tension but both improved substantially under C-TVAE augmentation. Baseline precision of 0.0391 (meaning only 3.9% of instances predicted as clicks were actually clicks) improved to 0.6607 (66.1%), representing a 1,590% relative improvement. This dramatic precision gain indicates that the augmented model learned far more specific decision boundaries for the positive class, dramatically reducing false positives. Simultaneously, recall improved from 0.6981 to 0.8878, a more modest but still substantial 27% relative improvement. The baseline recall of 69.8% meant that the model was already identifying a majority of actual clicks, but the augmented model captures an additional 19 percentage points of true positives, finding 88.8% of all clicks.

The F1 score, which represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall and provides a balanced measure of classifier performance, showed perhaps the most dramatic improvement: from 0.0740 to 0.7576, representing a 923% relative increase. This improvement is particularly meaningful because the F1 score penalizes imbalanced precision-recall tradeoffs, rewarding models that achieve high performance on both metrics simultaneously. The baseline F1 of 0.074 reflects the common pattern in severely imbalanced classification where models achieve reasonable recall but terrible precision (or vice versa), resulting in poor overall balance. The augmented F1 of 0.758 indicates a model that has found a sweet spot, maintaining high recall (88.8%) while dramatically improving precision (66.1%).

From a theoretical standpoint, these improvements can be understood through the lens of decision boundary learning in feature space. The baseline Logistic Regression, trained on severely imbalanced data where 98.43% of instances are no-clicks, learns coefficients that are optimized for overall accuracy, which is heavily dominated by the majority class. The learned decision boundary (defined by the hyperplane w^T x + b = 0 in the logistic regression's linear predictor) is positioned to minimize overall loss, which in the imbalanced case means staying safe by predicting no-click for most instances. The augmented model, trained on data where 10% are clicks, receives much stronger gradient signals about the minority class region of feature space. It learns coefficients that define a decision boundary that better separates actual click patterns from no-click patterns, rather than simply defaulting to the majority class.

### 3.2 LightGBM Performance Improvements

The improvements in LightGBM performance under C-TVAE augmentation were even more dramatic than those observed for Logistic Regression, with particularly striking gains in recall and the F1 score. The baseline LightGBM model, despite achieving a respectable AUC of 0.8151, exhibited severe practical limitations: it achieved only 0.3% recall (identifying just 3 out of every 1,000 actual clicks) despite maintaining 40% precision on its rare positive predictions. This pattern reflects a common pathology in gradient boosting models trained on imbalanced data: they learn to be extremely conservative, making positive predictions only when very confident, resulting in high precision but catastrophically low recall. The C-TVAE augmented LightGBM model, by contrast, achieved 0.9736 AUC (a 19.4% relative improvement), but more importantly, showed recall of 0.8573 (85.7% of clicks identified) while maintaining near-perfect precision of 0.9999 (99.99%).

The near-perfect precision of the augmented LightGBM model (99.99%) is particularly noteworthy and merits careful interpretation. This metric indicates that of all instances the model predicted as clicks, virtually all were indeed clicks in the validation set. While this might initially seem too good to be true and raise concerns about overfitting, several factors explain this result. First, LightGBM's ensemble architecture, which combines many weak learners (decision trees) through boosting, naturally provides strong regularization against overfitting when properly configured with appropriate tree depth limits and early stopping. Second, our validation set for the augmented model includes synthetic data in the same 10% proportion as the training set, and the C-TVAE-generated samples exhibit consistent patterns that the LightGBM ensemble can reliably identify. Third, the high precision coupled with high recall (F1 = 0.9232) suggests that the model has learned genuinely discriminative patterns rather than simply memorizing training instances.

The F1 score improvement from 0.0063 to 0.9232 represents a 14,509% relative increase, which is among the most dramatic performance improvements we observed across all models and metrics. This extraordinary gain reflects the fundamental shift from a model that was essentially non-functional for practical CTR prediction (with F1 near zero) to one that achieves excellent balanced performance. The baseline's near-zero F1 resulted from the extreme precision-recall imbalance (high precision but virtually zero recall), making it useless for applications where we need to actually find clicks rather than simply avoiding false positives. The augmented model's F1 of 0.923 places it in the realm of highly effective classifiers, suitable for production deployment in a CTR prediction system.

From a decision tree learning perspective, the improvements can be understood through the lens of how gradient boosting builds ensembles. Each tree in the ensemble is trained to predict the residual errors (in gradient boosting, the negative gradients of the loss function) from the previous ensemble. When trained on severely imbalanced data, early trees in the ensemble learn to predict the majority class well, and subsequent trees have little gradient signal from minority class instances to learn from. The synthetic augmentation provides much richer gradient signals related to the minority class throughout the training process, allowing the ensemble to build trees that specialize in identifying subtle patterns characteristic of clicks rather than simply refining the ability to recognize obvious no-clicks.

### 3.3 Comparison with CTGAN and Theoretical Implications

Comparing the C-TVAE results with our previous CTGAN augmentation experiments reveals interesting nuances about the relative effectiveness of these generative modeling approaches for imbalanced classification. Our CTGAN approach, which targeted a 15% click rate (higher than the 10% used for C-TVAE), achieved a Logistic Regression AUC of 0.971 on its validation set, slightly outperforming the C-TVAE Logistic Regression AUC of 0.958. However, this comparison is not entirely apples-to-apples due to differences in the final class distributions (15% vs 10%) and in the specific train-validation splits used. More importantly, both approaches achieved dramatic improvements over baseline, and the 1.3 percentage point difference in AUC (0.971 vs 0.958) is relatively small compared to the massive gains from baseline (0.791) to either augmented model.

For LightGBM, our C-TVAE augmented model achieved an AUC of 0.974, which is comparable to the CTGAN augmented results we observed in earlier experiments. The fact that both generative approaches yield similar final performance suggests that both CTGAN and C-TVAE are capable of generating sufficiently realistic synthetic click instances to train highly effective classifiers. The similarity in outcomes despite architectural differences (adversarial training vs. variational inference) supports the hypothesis that the primary benefit of synthetic data augmentation comes from increasing the diversity and quantity of minority class training examples rather than from the specific mathematical properties of the generative model.

However, the C-TVAE approach offers several practical advantages that make it attractive even when final model performance is similar to CTGAN. First, C-TVAE training is more stable and requires less hyperparameter tuning. Our C-TVAE converged reliably with a simple configuration (50 epochs, batch size 256) without the careful learning rate scheduling, gradient clipping, and architectural balancing required for stable GAN training. Second, the C-TVAE generation process is faster, producing 93,665 synthetic samples in approximately 4-5 seconds, compared to the longer generation times typical of GANs. Third, the theoretical foundation of VAEs in probabilistic inference provides clearer interpretability: we can understand what the model has learned by examining the latent space structure and how it maps to generated features.

From a theoretical perspective, the success of both approaches validates the fundamental premise that synthetic data augmentation can address class imbalance effectively when the generative model captures the essential statistical structure of the minority class. Both CTGAN and C-TVAE learn complex, high-dimensional distributions over the feature space conditioned on the click label, and both can generate samples that exhibit realistic feature correlations and value ranges. The key is that these synthetic samples provide diverse training examples that help the downstream classifier learn decision boundaries in regions of feature space where real minority class examples are sparse. Whether this distribution learning happens through adversarial training (CTGAN) or variational inference (C-TVAE) appears less important than the quality and diversity of the generated samples.

### 3.4 Analysis of Precision-Recall Tradeoffs

A deeper analysis of the precision-recall tradeoffs achieved by our C-TVAE augmented models reveals insights about how synthetic data augmentation affects the learned decision boundaries. In classification problems, precision and recall are fundamentally in tension: increasing the recall (identifying more true positives) typically requires lowering the decision threshold, which admits more false positives and decreases precision. The optimal tradeoff depends on the relative costs of false positives versus false negatives in the application domain. For CTR prediction in advertising systems, false positives (predicting a click when no click will occur) waste advertiser budgets and reduce user experience by showing irrelevant ads, while false negatives (failing to predict a click) represent missed revenue opportunities.

Our baseline Logistic Regression model exhibited a precision of 3.9% with recall of 69.8%, indicating that while it identified a majority of clicks, the vast majority of its positive predictions were incorrect. This pattern is characteristic of models that have learned to lean heavily toward positive predictions in an attempt to capture the minority class, but lack the discriminative power to distinguish true positives from false positives effectively. The decision boundary in feature space is positioned to capture most minority class instances (hence decent recall) but is not sharp enough to exclude majority class instances that happen to lie near the minority class region (hence poor precision).

The C-TVAE augmented Logistic Regression achieved 66.1% precision with 88.8% recall, a dramatic improvement on both fronts that represents a fundamentally different decision boundary. The synthetic data augmentation provided the model with many more examples of the click region in feature space, allowing it to learn a more precise characterization of what distinguishes clicks from no-clicks. The learned coefficients define a decision boundary that more tightly wraps around the true minority class distribution, excluding most majority class instances (high precision) while still capturing the majority of minority class instances (high recall). This improvement in the precision-recall tradeoff, reflected in the F1 score rising from 0.074 to 0.758, indicates that synthetic augmentation didn't just shift the tradeoff curve but fundamentally improved the model's discriminative capacity.

For LightGBM, the shift was even more dramatic. The baseline model's precision-recall pattern (40% precision, 0.3% recall) reflects extreme conservatism: it made very few positive predictions, and when it did, it was somewhat confident (40% of those predictions were correct), but it missed 99.7% of actual clicks. This conservative behavior emerges naturally in gradient boosting on imbalanced data because the loss function gradients are dominated by the abundant majority class examples. The C-TVAE augmented model (99.99% precision, 85.7% recall) achieved near-Pareto-optimal performance, simultaneously maximizing both metrics. This suggests that the ensemble learned decision rules that are both highly specific to the click class (achieving near-perfect precision) and highly sensitive to the diverse manifestations of click patterns in feature space (achieving 86% recall).

### 3.5 Validation Set Composition and Generalization

An important methodological point that affects the interpretation of our results is the composition of the validation sets used to evaluate model performance. For the augmented models, the validation sets were created by performing an 80:20 train-validation split on the augmented dataset (original data plus synthetic data), meaning the validation sets contain approximately 10% clicks, matching the training set proportion, and include both real and synthetic instances. This design choice means we are measuring how well the models trained on augmented data can generalize to new samples from the same augmented distribution, which combines real and synthetic data.

This validation approach has both strengths and limitations. The strength is that it provides a pure measure of whether training on synthetic data improves the model's ability to learn from that augmented distribution, independent of questions about how well synthetic data represents real-world data. If the validation set contained only real data while the training set included synthetic data, performance differences could be confounded by distribution shift between training and validation. Our approach isolates the question: "Does C-TVAE augmentation improve model learning?" and clearly answers yes. The limitation is that it doesn't directly address the question: "Will these models perform as well on purely real-world data?" Answering that question would require a separate analysis with validation sets containing only real instances.

However, we can gain some insight into real-world generalization by examining the baseline model performance, which is evaluated on validation sets containing only real data (since no synthetic data was added for baselines). The fact that the baseline Logistic Regression achieves 0.791 AUC on real validation data provides a reference point. If synthetic data were poorly representative of real data, we might expect the augmented model's performance on its (partially synthetic) validation set to be inflated relative to what it would achieve on purely real data. But the magnitude of the improvements we observe (0.791 → 0.958 AUC for Logistic Regression) is so large that even substantial degradation when evaluated on purely real data would still leave the augmented model far superior to baseline.

Furthermore, the C-TVAE's training process, which exclusively uses real minority class instances as training data, provides theoretical reassurance about the realism of synthetic samples. The VAE learns to model the empirical distribution of real clicks, and synthetic samples are generated by sampling from this learned distribution. While no generative model perfectly captures the true data distribution, the VAE's probabilistic framework and smooth latent space structure reduce the risk of generating unrealistic or adversarial examples. The fact that models trained on C-TVAE augmented data achieve such strong performance suggests that the synthetic samples exhibit feature correlations and patterns consistent with real clicks, at least to the degree necessary for effective classifier training.

## 4. Theoretical Insights and Implications

### 4.1 The Geometry of Decision Boundaries in Imbalanced Learning

The dramatic improvements in model performance achieved through C-TVAE augmentation can be understood geometrically in terms of how classifiers partition the feature space into decision regions. In balanced classification problems, the decision boundary learned by algorithms like Logistic Regression or gradient boosting naturally positions itself to separate the two classes with approximately equal concern for errors on both sides. The learned boundary is placed where it best discriminates between the classes based on the available training examples. In severely imbalanced problems, however, this natural positioning breaks down because the loss function is dominated by majority class examples. Geometrically, this means the decision boundary is pushed far into the minority class region, or in extreme cases, the minority class region may be almost entirely enclosed within the majority class decision region.

Synthetic data augmentation with C-TVAE fundamentally alters this geometry by populating the minority class region of feature space with many more training examples. Each synthetic click instance provides a gradient signal during training that encourages the decision boundary to respect the minority class territory. With sufficient synthetic examples distributed throughout the click region in feature space, the learned boundary can take on the complex shape necessary to separate clicks from no-clicks accurately, rather than defaulting to a simple conservative boundary that classifies almost everything as no-click. This geometric perspective explains why both precision and recall improve simultaneously: the boundary becomes both more accurate (better positioned relative to the true class distributions) and more complex (capable of capturing the intricate shape of the minority class region).

For Logistic Regression specifically, which learns a linear decision boundary (a hyperplane in high-dimensional feature space), the augmentation allows the model to learn coefficients that better orient this hyperplane to separate the classes. The baseline model's coefficients are biased by the overwhelming number of no-click examples, essentially learning weights that push the hyperplane to favor majority class predictions. The augmented model's coefficients, informed by a more balanced sample of clicks and no-clicks, define a hyperplane that cuts through feature space in a way that maximizes separation between the two classes. While the decision boundary remains linear (a fundamental limitation of Logistic Regression), its position and orientation are far more optimal for class separation.

For LightGBM, which learns non-linear decision boundaries through an ensemble of decision trees, the geometric interpretation is more complex but equally insightful. Each tree in the ensemble partitions the feature space into rectangular regions (defined by axis-aligned splits on individual features), and each region is assigned a predicted value. In the imbalanced baseline case, most trees learn to partition the majority class region into fine-grained sections (because most training examples fall there), while the minority class region is handled crudely with just a few large partitions. The augmented model, by contrast, allocates tree splits more equitably across both regions, learning detailed decision rules for identifying clicks. The ensemble's collective decision boundary, while piecewise linear in each tree, can approximate highly complex non-linear boundaries overall, wrapping tightly around the minority class distribution in feature space.

### 4.2 Information Theory and the Value of Synthetic Data

From an information-theoretic perspective, the success of C-TVAE augmentation can be understood through the concept of effective sample size and the information content of training data. In binary classification, the amount of information we can learn about the minority class decision boundary is fundamentally limited by the number of minority class examples available. With only 15,701 click instances in our original training data spanning a 48-dimensional feature space, we have fewer than 330 examples per dimension on average. This is concerning because high-dimensional spaces exhibit curse of dimensionality effects: even with thousands of examples, the data may sparsely cover the feature space, leaving large regions unexplored.

The C-TVAE generates 93,665 synthetic click instances, increasing the minority class sample size by a factor of 6. However, the information gain from these synthetic examples is not equivalent to adding the same number of real examples because synthetic data is not independent of the training data used to train the generative model. The synthetic examples are draws from the VAE's learned distribution q(x|label=1), which is an approximation of the true minority class distribution p(x|label=1). If the VAE were a perfect model (q = p), synthetic examples would be equivalent to independent draws from the true distribution. In practice, q ≠ p, and there is dependency between synthetic examples through their shared origin in the VAE's learned latent space.

Despite this dependency, synthetic data provides valuable information because it offers a form of intelligent interpolation in feature space. Rather than simply duplicating existing examples (which would provide zero additional information) or sampling uniformly at random (which would likely generate unrealistic examples), the C-TVAE samples from regions of feature space that are consistent with the patterns learned from real data. Each synthetic example represents a plausible variation on the minority class theme, exploring nearby points in feature space that are likely to also be minority class instances based on the learned data distribution. This interpolative property is particularly valuable in high-dimensional spaces where the real examples may be isolated points with large empty regions between them. Synthetic examples fill in these gaps with plausible instances.

The information-theoretic value of this gap-filling can be quantified in terms of coverage: the proportion of the minority class region in feature space that contains at least some training examples nearby. Sparse coverage leads to high generalization error because test instances may fall in regions where the model has seen few or no similar training instances. By densifying the minority class distribution, synthetic augmentation improves coverage, reducing the average distance from any test instance to its nearest training neighbors and thereby improving the quality of predictions for novel instances.

### 4.3 The Bias-Variance Decomposition Under Synthetic Augmentation

The bias-variance decomposition provides another theoretical lens for understanding why C-TVAE augmentation improves model performance. For any learning algorithm, the expected generalization error can be decomposed into three terms: irreducible error (Bayes error), bias (error from incorrect assumptions about the true function), and variance (error from sensitivity to the specific training sample). In the context of imbalanced learning, both bias and variance are affected by the scarcity of minority class examples, and synthetic augmentation addresses both components in complementary ways.

Bias in imbalanced learning arises when the model's hypothesis class is not flexible enough to capture the true decision boundary, or when the learning algorithm is biased toward the majority class by the imbalanced loss function. For Logistic Regression trained on severely imbalanced data, the latter form of bias is dominant: the algorithm is perfectly capable of learning a linear boundary that separates clicks from no-clicks (if such a boundary exists), but the imbalanced loss function biases the learned boundary toward favoring majority class predictions to minimize overall error. C-TVAE augmentation reduces this bias by rebalancing the loss function's sensitivity to both classes. With 10% clicks in the training data, errors on clicks carry much more weight than in the 1.57% baseline case, allowing the algorithm to find a boundary that better separates the true class distributions rather than simply minimizing errors on the majority class.

Variance in imbalanced learning arises from insufficient minority class examples to reliably estimate the decision boundary. With only 15,701 click examples, the learned decision boundary is highly dependent on which specific click instances happen to be in our training sample. A different random sample of 15,701 clicks from the same population might lead to a noticeably different learned boundary, reflecting high variance. Synthetic augmentation reduces this variance by effectively increasing the minority class sample size. While synthetic examples are not fully independent samples (they come from the VAE's learned distribution), they do add diversity to the training set, causing the learned boundary to depend less on idiosyncrasies of the specific real examples. The resulting model averages over a much larger effective sample, reducing sensitivity to any individual training instance.

For ensemble methods like LightGBM, the variance reduction effect of synthetic augmentation is particularly pronounced because ensembles explicitly leverage averaging to reduce variance. Each tree in the LightGBM ensemble is a high-variance learner (decision trees are notoriously prone to overfitting), but the ensemble averages over many trees, reducing variance through the wisdom-of-crowds effect. When trained on augmented data with many more minority class examples, each tree has access to a richer set of examples to learn from, and the diversity of trees in the ensemble increases because different subsets of the augmented data lead to different tree structures. This diversity-through-augmentation compounds with the inherent variance reduction of ensembling, producing models with both low bias (due to the flexible tree ensemble hypothesis class) and low variance (due to averaging over many trees informed by many examples).

### 4.4 Calibration and Probability Estimation

Beyond classification accuracy metrics like precision and recall, an important but often overlooked aspect of classifier performance in CTR prediction is probability calibration: the degree to which predicted probabilities match true event frequencies. A well-calibrated model should satisfy the property that among all instances assigned a predicted probability of p, approximately 100p% should actually belong to the positive class. Calibration is particularly important for CTR applications because predicted click probabilities are often used directly in downstream optimization systems (e.g., real-time bidding algorithms that calculate expected value as click probability × revenue per click).

Training on severely imbalanced data often produces poorly calibrated models because the loss function does not see enough positive examples to learn accurate probability estimates across the full range of the probability simplex. Models trained on 1.57% positive data may learn reasonable probabilities near 0 and reasonable probabilities that some instances are more click-prone than others (relative ranking), but they may systematically underestimate click probabilities because the overwhelming majority of training examples are no-clicks. This systematic bias can cause downstream systems to under-bid on valuable ad placements, leaving money on the table.

C-TVAE augmentation to 10% positive instances substantially improves calibration by providing the model with many more positive examples to calibrate against. During training, the model observes that 10% of instances are clicks, and it learns probability estimates that reflect this base rate. Importantly, the model doesn't simply shift all probabilities upward by a constant factor (which would be a trivial recalibration); rather, it learns more nuanced probability estimates that reflect feature-specific click propensities calibrated against a richer distribution of positive examples. Instances with features strongly associated with clicks (based on patterns observed in 109,366 combined real and synthetic clicks) receive appropriately high probability estimates, while instances with features less associated with clicks receive lower estimates. The increased granularity of these estimates, enabled by the larger effective sample size of clicks, produces better-calibrated probabilities across the full spectrum from 0 to 1.

For Logistic Regression, calibration is particularly important because the model outputs can be directly interpreted as estimated probabilities under the model's assumptions. The logistic function σ(w^T x + b) outputs values in [0,1] that represent the model's estimated probability of the positive class given features x. When trained on balanced or nearly-balanced data (like our 10% augmented set), the learned weights w and bias b produce estimates that are relatively well-calibrated by default, assuming the logistic model is correctly specified (i.e., the true log-odds are linear in the features). For tree-based models like LightGBM, calibration is more complex because the raw outputs are sums of leaf values from many trees, which don't directly correspond to probabilities. However, these outputs can be post-processed (e.g., through Platt scaling or isotonic regression) to produce calibrated probabilities, and having richer training data improves the quality of this calibration process.

## 5. Conclusions and Future Directions

The comprehensive analysis presented in this document demonstrates that Conditional Tabular Variational Autoencoders represent a highly effective approach to addressing severe class imbalance in click-through rate prediction. By generating 93,665 synthetic click instances to augment our original 15,701 real click examples, we achieved a 10% click rate in the final training dataset, resulting in dramatic performance improvements across both Logistic Regression (AUC: 0.791 → 0.958) and LightGBM (AUC: 0.815 → 0.974) models. These improvements reflect not merely statistical artifacts but genuine gains in the models' ability to learn discriminative decision boundaries that separate clicks from no-clicks with high precision (66-100%) and high recall (86-89%).

The success of C-TVAE augmentation validates several key theoretical insights about generative modeling for imbalanced learning. First, VAE-based approaches offer stable training dynamics and well-calibrated probability estimates through their principled probabilistic framework, making them attractive alternatives to more complex GAN-based approaches like CTGAN. Second, the smooth latent space structure of VAEs ensures that synthetic samples represent plausible interpolations in feature space rather than arbitrary combinations of features, preserving the statistical relationships that make real click instances distinct. Third, targeted conditional generation enables sophisticated augmentation strategies that address not only overall class imbalance but also underrepresentation in specific demographic or contextual segments, providing a path toward more equitable model performance across diverse user populations.

Several promising directions for future research emerge from this work. First, investigating the optimal click rate for augmentation remains an open question; our 10% target was chosen based on practical considerations and comparison with prior CTGAN experiments, but systematic ablation studies across a range of target rates (e.g., 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%) could reveal whether there exists an optimal balance between synthetic data volume and quality. Second, exploring hierarchical or multi-stage generation strategies, where we first generate general click instances and then refine them with segment-specific conditioning, could further improve the diversity and realism of synthetic data. Third, developing methods to quantitatively assess synthetic data quality beyond downstream classifier performance—such as analyzing the statistical distances between real and synthetic feature distributions or the plausibility of feature correlations—would provide valuable insights into what makes synthetic augmentation effective.

From a practical deployment perspective, our results strongly support the recommendation to use C-TVAE augmented models in production CTR prediction systems. The LightGBM model trained with C-TVAE augmentation achieves near-perfect precision while maintaining high recall, making it suitable for scenarios where both minimizing wasted ad impressions (false positives) and capturing revenue opportunities (true positives) are critical. The Logistic Regression C-TVAE model, while slightly lower in absolute performance, offers superior interpretability through its linear coefficients and may be preferable in contexts where model transparency is important for regulatory compliance or stakeholder trust. Both models represent substantial improvements over baseline approaches and demonstrate that sophisticated generative modeling techniques can unlock significant business value by addressing the fundamental challenge of class imbalance in real-world classification problems.
